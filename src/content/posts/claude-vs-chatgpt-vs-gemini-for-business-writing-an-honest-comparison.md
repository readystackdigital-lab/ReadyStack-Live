Every Australian business owner we work with is now paying for at least one AI assistant, and most have no idea whether they picked the right one. The comparison articles out there are mostly benchmark charts and affiliate links, which tells you nothing useful about whether the thing will write a decent client proposal.

We use all three at ReadyStack, daily, for real client work: proposals, service pages, email sequences, contracts, and technical documentation. This is what we've actually found.

## The short answer

If you only want one, here's the honest summary.

Choose Claude if the writing itself is the point. Long-form content, proposals, anything client-facing where tone matters, anything where you'll be editing heavily and want a strong first draft rather than a starting sketch.

Choose ChatGPT if you want the widest capability surface. It does more things than the other two: images, voice, data analysis, agentic tasks that click through websites. If you want one subscription to cover everything, this is it.

Choose Gemini if your business runs on Google Workspace. The integration into Gmail, Docs, and Sheets is genuinely useful in a way that copy-pasting between a chat window and your documents never quite is.

## Writing quality: the part that actually matters

This is subjective, and anyone claiming objectivity here is selling something. But after a year of using all three for client deliverables, some consistent patterns emerge.

Claude produces the most natural business prose. It's less prone to the tells that make AI writing obvious: the triadic lists, the "in today's fast-paced world" openers, the relentless hedging, the em-dash addiction. It also takes tone instructions more literally, so "write this like a Sydney accountant talking to a client they've known for ten years" produces something closer to that than a generic professional register. For long documents, it holds a consistent voice across thousands of words better than the other two.

Where Claude is weaker: it can be verbose if you don't rein it in, and it sometimes over-qualifies when you want a confident statement.

ChatGPT is the most versatile and the most generic by default. Out of the box, its business writing has a recognisable house style: enthusiastic, structured, heavy on headers and bullet points, slightly breathless. The good news is that it's very steerable. With a solid custom instruction set or a well-built project, it will write in almost any voice you specify. The bad news is that most people never do this, which is why so much AI-written LinkedIn content reads identically.

Where ChatGPT wins clearly: brainstorming, ideation, and any task where you want twenty options rather than one good one.

Gemini is the strongest on factual grounding and the weakest on voice. Its search integration means it's less likely to confidently invent a statistic, which matters for anything with numbers in it. But its prose tends toward the encyclopaedic. It explains rather than persuades. For a market research summary, that's exactly right. For a landing page, it's not.

Where Gemini wins clearly: research-heavy writing, summarising long documents, and anything where you need to work across a large volume of source material.

## Workflow and integration

This is where the real differences live, and it's the factor most comparison articles ignore.

Gemini is embedded in Google Workspace. If your business runs on Gmail and Google Docs, Gemini appears in the sidebar of the document you're already writing. It can pull from your Drive, reference your emails, and draft replies in Gmail without you leaving the tab. For a small professional services firm on Workspace, this friction reduction is worth more than a marginal quality difference.

ChatGPT has the broadest integration surface. It connects to a long list of third-party apps, handles voice conversation well, generates images, and has agentic features that can complete multi-step tasks across connected tools. If your business uses a scattered stack of different SaaS tools, this breadth matters.

Claude sits somewhere in between, with a specific strength in project-based work. You can set up a project containing your brand guidelines, past proposals, tone-of-voice document, and service descriptions, and everything you write in that project draws on it automatically. For an agency or consultancy producing a lot of similar documents, this is the single most useful feature any of the three offers. Claude also has desktop tools for working across your own files, and connects to Google Drive and Slack.

## Pricing (verified August 2026)

All prices below are USD. Australian buyers should assume GST and exchange rate movement on top, which realistically puts a $20 USD plan somewhere around $32 to $35 AUD per month depending on the day.

Claude: Free tier available. Pro is $20 per month, or $17 per month billed annually. Max plans sit at $100 and $200 per month for heavy users. Team plans start at $25 per seat per month, with a premium seat tier at $125. Enterprise runs at $20 per seat plus usage billed at API rates.

ChatGPT: Free tier available. There's a cheap Go plan at $8 per month, Plus at $20 per month, and two Pro tiers at $100 and $200. Business seats are $25 per user per month, dropping to around $20 on annual billing, with a two-seat minimum. Enterprise is custom.

Gemini: Free tier available. Google AI Plus is $4.99 per month, Google AI Pro is $19.99, and Google AI Ultra starts at $99.99 with a higher tier at $199.99. Consumer plans bundle Google One storage. Separately, Gemini is included in Google Workspace Business Standard and above, which is often the relevant path for a business already paying for Workspace.

A note on the Workspace path: if you're already on Google Workspace Business Standard, you may already have Gemini included at no extra cost. A surprising number of Australian SMBs are paying for a separate AI subscription while sitting on one they've already bought.

## Privacy and data handling for Australian businesses

This deserves more attention than it usually gets, particularly for professional services firms handling client information.

The general rule across all three: consumer plans have weaker data protections than business plans. Business and enterprise tiers on all three providers exclude your data from model training by default. Consumer tiers vary, and settings change.

For Australian firms with confidentiality obligations, the practical guidance is:

Use a business or team plan, not a personal one, if client information will touch the tool. The price difference is small and the contractual position is meaningfully different.

Check data residency if you have specific obligations. None of the three guarantees Australian data residency on standard consumer plans. Enterprise arrangements may offer more control.

Don't paste identifiable client information into any AI tool unless you've confirmed your plan's terms and your professional obligations allow it. This applies as much to a "quick summary of these meeting notes" as to anything formal.

Update your privacy policy if AI tools process personal information you've collected. The Privacy Act reforms have raised the stakes on transparency about how personal information is handled.

## How to actually decide

Ignore benchmarks. They measure things that don't correlate well with whether the tool writes a good proposal for your business.

Instead, run this test. Take a document you've already written and are happy with: a proposal that won, a service page that converts, an email that got a reply. Give each assistant the same brief that produced it, plus a sample of your writing, and compare the drafts against your original.

You'll know within an hour which one fits your voice. That's the only benchmark that matters, and it costs nothing because all three have free tiers.

Then, once you've picked, invest an hour in setup. Write a proper tone-of-voice document. Load your service descriptions, pricing structure, and two or three past deliverables. Every one of the three gets dramatically better with context, and almost nobody does this. The gap between a well-configured assistant and a cold chat window is much bigger than the gap between the three products.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which AI is best for writing business proposals?

Claude generally produces the most natural, client-ready prose for proposals, and its project feature lets you load past proposals and brand guidelines so drafts match your existing voice. ChatGPT is a strong alternative if you want to generate several structural options quickly.

### Is Gemini free if I already pay for Google Workspace?

Gemini is included in Google Workspace Business Standard and higher tiers, so many businesses already have access without a separate subscription. Check your current Workspace plan before paying for a standalone AI subscription.

### Can Australian businesses use these tools with client information?

With care. Business and enterprise tiers exclude your data from model training by default, which consumer tiers may not. None of the three offers Australian data residency on standard consumer plans. If you have professional confidentiality obligations, review your plan terms and your professional body's guidance before putting client information into any AI tool.

### How much do these AI assistants cost in Australian dollars?

All three are priced in USD. The common $20 USD individual tier works out to roughly $32 to $35 AUD per month once exchange rate and GST are accounted for, though this moves with the dollar. Gemini's entry tier is cheaper at around $5 USD, and all three have functional free tiers.

### Will Google penalise my website for AI-written content?

Google penalises unhelpful content, not AI-assisted content specifically. Its guidance targets content produced primarily to manipulate rankings rather than to help readers. AI-drafted content that is accurate, genuinely useful, and edited by someone who knows the subject ranks fine. Unedited AI content published at volume does not.

### Do I need more than one AI subscription?

Most small businesses don't. Pick the one that fits your primary use case and workflow. Running multiple only makes sense when you have genuinely different needs across writing, research, and image generation, and when the time saved clearly exceeds the cost.

## The bigger point

The tool matters less than the setup. A business owner who spends an hour building a proper context document and learns to brief clearly will get better output from the free tier of any of these than someone paying $200 a month and typing one-line prompts.

And whichever you choose, the editing step is not optional. Every piece of AI-drafted content that goes out under your business name needs a human who understands the subject to check the facts, cut the filler, and make sure it sounds like you. That's the part no subscription tier solves.
