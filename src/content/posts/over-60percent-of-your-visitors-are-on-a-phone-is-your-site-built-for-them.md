Most of the people looking at your website right now are holding a phone. For a typical Australian professional services business, mobile traffic sits somewhere between 55% and 70% of all visits, and for anything people search for on the go (a plumber, a physio, a cafe, a lawyer they need today) it's often higher.

Here's the problem. Almost every website is designed on a desktop, by someone sitting at a desktop, and the phone version gets a quick check at the end. That's backwards. The version most of your customers see is the one that got the least attention.

This post is about telling whether your own site actually works on a phone, and fixing the things that matter most if it doesn't. No jargon, and you can do most of the checking yourself in about ten minutes.

---

## Why this matters more than it used to

Two things changed that make mobile non-negotiable rather than nice-to-have.

First, Google switched to mobile-first indexing years ago, and it's now universal. Google looks at the mobile version of your site to decide how to rank you, even for searches made on a desktop. If your mobile site is slow or broken, your desktop rankings suffer too.

Second, buyer behaviour shifted. People research on their phone in spare moments: on the train, in a waiting room, on the couch. They form an impression of your business in seconds, and if the site is frustrating to use, they leave and tap the next result. On mobile, the next result is one thumb-flick away.

So a bad mobile experience isn't just an inconvenience for some visitors. It quietly costs you rankings and leads at the same time, and most owners never see it happening because they check their own site on a laptop.

## The ten-minute self-test

Pick up your phone and open your own website. Not the admin view, the actual public site, ideally on mobile data rather than wifi so you feel the real load speed. Then check each of these.

Does it load in under three seconds? Count it out. If you're still staring at a blank or half-built screen at three seconds, that's the single biggest problem to fix, because most people won't wait.

Can you read the text without zooming? If you're pinching to enlarge anything, the font is too small. Body text should be comfortably readable held at normal distance.

Can you tap every button and link easily with your thumb? Try it one-handed. If you're mis-tapping, hitting the wrong link, or having to aim carefully, the tap targets are too small or too close together.

Does anything run off the side of the screen? Scroll down the whole page. If you have to swipe sideways to see the rest of a section, or a photo or table pokes past the edge, the layout isn't adapting properly.

Can you find your phone number and tap to call it in one or two taps? For a service business this is the whole game. If a customer has to hunt for it, copy it, and paste it into their dialer, you've added friction at the exact moment they wanted to contact you.

Is the menu usable? Tap it open, tap it closed, try to reach every main page. A menu that's hard to open or covers the whole screen with no obvious way out is a common and quiet killer.

Can you complete your contact form on the phone? Actually fill it in. Do the right keyboards appear (number pad for phone, email keyboard for email)? Is anything hard to tap or type? Does it submit cleanly?

Do popups or cookie banners trap you? On a small screen, a popup with a tiny or missing close button can block the entire page. If you can't dismiss it easily, neither can your customers.

If your site fails three or more of these, it's costing you real business. The good news is that the fixes are well understood, and most are one-time jobs.

## The fixes that matter most, in order

Not everything is equally important. If you can only address a few things, address these first.

### 1. Speed

Speed is the foundation, because nothing else matters if people leave before the page loads. On mobile, the usual culprits are oversized images, too much code loading upfront, and cheap hosting.

The highest-impact fix for most sites is images. Photos exported straight from a camera or phone are often several megabytes each, and a page with five of them can be enormous. Properly sized and compressed, those same images can be a fraction of the size with no visible quality loss. If your site is slow and image-heavy, this alone can transform it.

Beyond images, modern hosting and a lean build make a large difference. This is where the underlying technology of your site starts to matter, and it's why a heavy page builder with a dozen plugins struggles on mobile in a way a lean static site doesn't.

### 2. Tap targets and readable text

These are cheap to fix and immediately felt. Buttons and links need to be large enough to tap confidently with a thumb, with enough space around them that you don't hit the wrong one. Text needs to be big enough to read without zooming.

If your site was built on a modern responsive framework, this is usually a matter of adjustment rather than rebuild. If tapping and reading are genuinely painful across the whole site, that often signals an older or non-responsive build that needs more serious work.

### 3. Click-to-call and easy contact

For any business where people phone you, your number should be tappable and easy to find on mobile, ideally visible near the top and repeated at the bottom. A tap should open the dialer with your number ready to go.

The same logic applies to your address (tapping it should open maps) and your email. Every extra step between a ready customer and contacting you is a chance to lose them.

### 4. The menu

Mobile navigation should be obvious to open, obvious to close, and should let someone reach any main page quickly. Keep the number of top-level items small. A customer on a phone wants to get somewhere, not study a sitemap.

### 5. Forms

Contact and enquiry forms are where mobile friction turns directly into lost leads. Keep the number of fields to the minimum you actually need. Make sure the correct keyboard appears for each field. Make the submit button large and unmissable. Every additional field measurably reduces the number of people who finish.

### 6. Popups and banners

If you use popups or a cookie banner, test them on a real phone. They must be easy to dismiss with an obvious, appropriately sized close button. A popup that traps a mobile visitor doesn't just annoy them, it can make the whole site unusable and send them straight back to Google.

## Responsive is not the same as mobile-friendly

Here's a distinction that trips up a lot of business owners. Almost every website today is technically "responsive", meaning the layout rearranges itself to fit a phone screen. Your web person may well have told you the site is responsive, and they're probably telling the truth.

But responsive just means it fits. It doesn't mean it's good. A site can shrink neatly onto a phone and still have text that's too small, buttons too close together, a five megabyte hero image, and a contact form that's painful to complete. Responsive is the floor, not the ceiling.

The real question isn't "does my site fit on a phone". It's "is my site genuinely pleasant and fast to use on a phone". Those are different standards, and the gap between them is where leads quietly leak away.

---

## A note on how sites get built

Why are so many sites weak on mobile despite everyone knowing mobile matters? It comes down to how they're made.

When a site is designed desktop-first, the phone version is a compromise squeezed out of a layout that was never meant for a small screen. When a site is built mobile-first, the phone experience is designed properly from the start and the desktop version expands out from it. The second approach produces better mobile results, and it's the approach a good modern build takes as standard.

This is also where the underlying technology shows. Lean, modern builds on fast hosting pass mobile performance checks comfortably. Heavy builds loaded with plugins and page-builder overhead tend to struggle on phones no matter how much tuning you throw at them. If your site is persistently slow on mobile and image compression hasn't fixed it, the build itself may be the limiting factor.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I check if my website is mobile-friendly?

Open your public site on your own phone using mobile data, and run through the practical checks: does it load in under three seconds, can you read text without zooming, can you tap every button easily with a thumb, does anything run off the screen, and can you find and tap your phone number quickly. Google Search Console also reports mobile usability issues for your site.

### What percentage of website traffic is mobile in Australia?

It varies by industry, but for most Australian small businesses mobile makes up roughly 55% to 70% of website visits. Businesses people search for while out and about tend to see even higher mobile shares.

### Does mobile-friendliness affect Google rankings?

Yes. Google uses the mobile version of your site to determine rankings for all searches, including desktop ones, through mobile-first indexing. A slow or broken mobile site can lower your rankings everywhere.

### What's the most important mobile fix for a small business site?

Speed, and within that, image size. Oversized images are the most common cause of slow mobile pages, and properly compressing them often produces the biggest single improvement with no visible loss of quality.

### My developer said my site is responsive. Isn't that enough?

Responsive means the layout adapts to fit a phone, which is necessary but not sufficient. A responsive site can still be slow, hard to read, and awkward to tap. The goal is a site that is genuinely fast and pleasant to use on a phone, which is a higher standard than simply fitting the screen.

### Should my website be designed for mobile first?

For most small businesses, yes. Designing mobile-first means the experience most of your visitors get is the one built with the most care, rather than a compromise squeezed out of a desktop layout.

If most of your visitors are on a phone, the phone version of your site isn't the afterthought. It's the main event. Yet it's almost always the part that got the least attention, because it's the part the business owner and the designer see least.
